What the category marks mean

    The marks carry the category and are drawn in one stroke weight so they read as a set. Colour is not part of them: on this site colour means strain and era, so a mark is legible in plain black on white.

      Data sources and method

      This is an independent, public-interest tracker of avian influenza in Australia, with the wider world for context. Australian detections come automatically from the FAO EMPRES-i+ animal disease system, which aggregates the official WOAH notifications Australian agencies issue. United States wild-bird detections come from USDA APHIS, and global human-case figures from the WHO via Our World in Data. Data refreshes daily.

      The FAO feed follows WOAH notifications, which arrive after an Australian announcement, so the number of detections plotted here can trail the Commonwealth’s own figure. The page therefore reads the official national count straight from the Department of Agriculture’s H5 bird flu updates and national campaign page and shows it beside the mapped total. Those pages publish place names without coordinates, so they inform the counts and never add points to the map: a guessed coordinate on a public health map is worse than a missing one. A nationally significant first, such as a state’s first detection, may be added by hand with its primary source attached until the automated feed catches up.

          Further reference

            Notes and caveats

            • Records are grouped by the event they belong to and are never added together.
            • Coordinates follow the source. Some events are placed at a town or region, and a few resolve only to a state.
            • Not every Western Australian detection is itemised in public data, so counts follow what official sources publish.

            To report sick or dead wild birds, call the Emergency Animal Disease Hotline on 1800 675 888. Do not touch or move sick or dead birds.

            Not medical advice. Figures are aggregated from public official reports and may lag or be revised by the source agencies. The risk to the general public is currently assessed as low by Australian authorities. For guidance, consult the Australian CDC, DAFF, or your state health department.
